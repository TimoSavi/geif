# GEIF: Continuous Metric Depth & Eliminating Density Bias

## 1. The Anatomy of Issue 4: The Classic "Density Bias"

In classic Isolation Forest (Liu et al., 2008) and Extended Isolation Forest (Hariri et al., 2019), tree traversal depth is measured purely as a **discrete count of integer edge hops**:
$$\text{Root} = 0 \to \text{Child} = 1 \to \text{Grandchild} = 2 \dots$$

This integer counting creates a catastrophic flaw known as the **Density Bias**:

```text
       Cluster A (Dense Core)                     Cluster B (Diffuse Cloud)
       30 tightly packed points                   500 loosely scattered points

              * * * *                                *       *        *
             * * * * *                                   *        *
              * * * *                             *          *         *
       Isolates in ~5 hops                        Isolates in ~9 hops
       Path Length h = 5.0                        Path Length h = 9.0
```

### The Inverted Anomaly Paradox
In classic iForest:
* **Cluster A:** Completely isolates in $\log_2(30) \approx 4.9$ hops.
* **Cluster B:** Takes $\log_2(500) \approx 9.0$ hops.
* **Classic iForest Verdict:**
  $$\text{Score} = 2^{-h / c(n)}$$
  Classic iForest scores Cluster A as **`0.68` (Anomalous)** and Cluster B as **`0.38` (Nominal Inlier)**!

Classic iForest falsely classifies the tight, dense cluster as *more anomalous* than the loose, scattered cloud simply because Cluster A had fewer sample points. Classic iForest measures **sample cardinality**, not physical spatial density!

## 2. The GEIF Solution: Generator Separation as a Density Probe

In GEIF, every split is generated by two actual sample points: $A, B \in S_v$.

The normalized generator separation is:
$$\delta = \|\tilde{B} - \tilde{A}\| = \sqrt{\sum_{j=1}^D \left(\frac{B_j - A_j}{\text{span}_j}\right)^2}$$

Physically, $\delta$ is an intrinsic, local spatial probe:
* **Inside a dense cluster:** Samples are packed tightly $\implies \delta$ is microscopic ($\delta \approx 0.01$).
* **Inside a diffuse cloud:** Samples are far apart $\implies \delta$ is moderate ($\delta \approx 0.30$).
* **Between separate clusters:** Samples are distant $\implies \delta$ is large ($\delta \approx 0.80$).

GEIF extracts local spatial density **for free** from the difference vector, without needing expensive $k$-nearest-neighbor graphs or Kernel Density Estimation (KDE).

## 3. Formulating Continuous Metric Depth ($\Delta H$)

Instead of adding a flat $+1$ integer edge hop, traversing a GEIF node accumulates a **continuous metric depth increment $\Delta H(\delta)$**:

$$H \leftarrow H + \Delta H(\delta)$$

### 3.1 The Logarithmic Information-Theoretic Increment
Partitioning space between two samples at distance $\delta$ resolves spatial information proportional to the scale of the cut:

$$\Delta H(\delta) = 1.0 + \alpha \cdot \ln\left(1 + \frac{\delta_{\text{nominal}}}{\delta}\right)$$

Where:
* $1.0$ is the guaranteed baseline hop increment.
* $\delta_{\text{nominal}}$ is the mean sample spacing across the forest.
* $\alpha \ge 0$ is the density scaling coefficient (default: $\alpha = 1.0$).

### 3.2 Behavior Across Density Regimes

| Spatial Regime | Generator Distance $\delta$ | Density Ratio $\frac{\delta_{\text{nominal}}}{\delta}$ | Increment $\Delta H$ | Accumulation Speed |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Inter-Cluster Cut** | $\approx 0.80$ (Huge) | $\approx 0.12$ | **$\approx 1.1$** | Very Slow (Sparse) |
| **Diffuse Cloud** | $\approx 0.25$ (Wide) | $\approx 0.40$ | **$\approx 1.3$** | Slow |
| **Nominal Cluster** | $\approx 0.10$ (Nominal) | $\approx 1.00$ | **$\approx 1.7$** | Medium |
| **Dense Core** | $\approx 0.01$ (Packed) | $\approx 10.0$ | **$\approx 3.4$** | Rapid |
| **Ultra-Dense Spike** | $\approx 0.001$ (Extreme) | $\approx 100.0$ | **$\approx 5.6$** | Explosive (Ultra-Inlier) |

## 4. Resolving the Dense vs. Diffuse Benchmark

Now re-evaluate the 30-sample tight cluster vs. the 500-sample loose cloud under GEIF:

### Cluster A (30 samples, dense $\delta \approx 0.01$):
* Number of splits to isolate: $\approx 5$
* Metric increment per split: $\Delta H \approx 3.4$
* **Accumulated Continuous Metric Depth:**
  $$H_A = 5 \times 3.4 = \mathbf{17.0}$$

### Cluster B (500 samples, diffuse $\delta \approx 0.25$):
* Number of splits to isolate: $\approx 9$
* Metric increment per split: $\Delta H \approx 1.3$
* **Accumulated Continuous Metric Depth:**
  $$H_B = 9 \times 1.3 = \mathbf{11.7}$$

### The Final GEIF Scores:
$$H_A (17.0) > H_B (11.7)$$
$$\text{Score}_A = 1.0 - \frac{17.0}{H_{\max}} \approx \mathbf{0.150000} \quad (\text{Deep Nominal Inlier})$$
$$\text{Score}_B = 1.0 - \frac{11.7}{H_{\max}} \approx \mathbf{0.415000} \quad (\text{Moderate Periphery})$$

**The density bias is completely cured!** The dense cluster is recognized as significantly more nominal than the loose cloud.

## 5. Connecting to "Zero Kelvin": Calibrating $H_{\max}$

Earlier, we established that a score of `0.000000` must behave like **Zero Kelvin**: an absolute lower bound that inliers approach but never reach, leaving headroom for unobserved optimal inliers.

### The Calibration Rule:
1. At the conclusion of forest training, evaluate the continuous metric depth of all training samples:
   $$H_{\text{train, max}} = \max_{x \in X} H(x)$$
2. Set $H_{\max}$ with a headroom multiplier $\kappa > 1.0$ (default: $\kappa = 1.25$ to $1.50$):
   $$H_{\max} = \kappa \cdot H_{\text{train, max}}$$
3. The deepest inlier observed in training receives:
   $$\text{Score}(x_{\text{best}}) = 1.0 - \frac{H_{\text{train, max}}}{1.25 \cdot H_{\text{train, max}}} = 1.0 - 0.80 = \mathbf{0.200000}$$

### Why This Is Mathematically Pure:
* If future test data contains a point that is even more centered and dense than any training sample, its depth $H$ can rise to $1.1 \times H_{\text{train, max}}$.
* Its score drops smoothly to $1.0 - \frac{1.10}{1.25} = \mathbf{0.120000}$.
* It smoothly approaches $0.0$, but never crosses below zero.
* The system maintains continuous headroom forever for new optimal inliers.
